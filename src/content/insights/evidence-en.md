An online brand concern often arrives as a screenshot: a suspicious shop, an unfamiliar profile, or an advertisement using your identity. A useful report connects that observation to the brand owner and explains the concern with specific facts. Start by separating the type of issue from the evidence supporting it.

## Establish the legitimate brand reference

Record the owner’s legal name, the exact trademark or brand identifier, and the official website and social profiles. Where relevant, include the trademark registration number, jurisdiction, and goods or services covered. Explain any difference between the trademark owner and the organization making the report.

Keep evidence of authorization available if an employee, agency, or other representative is handling the matter. Do not assume that access to a marketing account establishes authority to make an intellectual property report.

Trademark infringement is a legal question involving more than a matching word or logo. The USPTO explains the role of valid rights, priority, and likelihood of consumer confusion in an infringement claim. See its [official overview of trademark infringement](https://www.uspto.gov/page/about-trademark-infringement). Obtain qualified legal advice where the rights or the use are disputed.

## Identify the reported item

Save a direct URL for each listing, advertisement, profile, or website page. Include visible account identifiers, seller names, and the date and time of observation. A profile URL and a particular product listing may both be useful, but they serve different purposes; label them separately.

Capture enough context to show how the brand appears. Preserve the page title, product description, claims of affiliation, or account biography when relevant. Keep the unedited capture and use a separate annotated copy for comparisons.

## Match the evidence to the concern

| Concern                         | Useful evidence                                                      | Avoid assuming                              |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Impersonation                   | Official identity references and the account’s claims of affiliation | Every similar name is impersonation         |
| Suspected counterfeit promotion | Specific product discrepancies, seller context, and brand records    | A low price proves a product is counterfeit |
| Trademark misuse                | The mark as displayed, its context, and relevant rights records      | Every mention of a brand violates rights    |

Describe what you can observe and what still needs verification. If your product team identifies a discrepancy, record the explanation and the reference used. A photograph may support a concern but may not allow reliable authentication of physical goods.

## Include relevant permissions and history

Check whether the seller, distributor, or account is known to your organization. Include relevant authorization or licensing context, even if it complicates the initial concern. An incomplete account of permissions can lead to a misleading report.

Preserve previous report references and provider replies. If you report a repeated appearance, connect the new URL to the earlier record while making clear that it is a new observation. Avoid treating several unrelated accounts as one operator without supporting evidence.

## Prepare a focused handoff

Put the rights records, URL list, captures, and explanation in a clearly organized folder. Maintain a short index so a reviewer can find each item. Share only relevant information, limiting unnecessary personal data. Keep a separate log of what was actually submitted and when.
