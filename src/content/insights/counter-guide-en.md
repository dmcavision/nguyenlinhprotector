A DMCA counter-notification is a formal response from a subscriber whose material was removed or disabled after a copyright notice. It is intended for a situation in which the subscriber believes the removal resulted from a mistake or misidentification. It is not a general appeal for every content-moderation decision, and it should not be submitted merely because the removal is inconvenient.

This guide explains how to organize the record before deciding whether to proceed. It provides general educational information, not a conclusion about infringement or advice for a particular dispute.

## Confirm why the material was removed

Start with the message from the online service provider. Confirm that it refers to a copyright notice and identify the exact material, account, URL, case number, and date involved. A platform may remove content for community rules, trademark concerns, privacy, contractual restrictions, or other reasons that do not use the section 512 counter-notification process.

Preserve the provider's full notice and any attachments. Save a copy of the material as it appeared before removal, the former URL, publication date, account information, and later correspondence. If the provider supplied its own counter-notification form, preserve a blank copy or screenshots of the required fields before entering information.

## Identify the basis for mistake or misidentification

The statutory process asks the subscriber to state, under penalty of perjury, a good-faith belief that the material was removed or disabled because of mistake or misidentification. Organize facts that bear directly on that belief. These might include a license, written permission, proof that the wrong material or URL was identified, evidence that you created the material, or context you want qualified counsel to assess.

Do not treat a disagreement with the complainant as proof of mistake. Ownership, license scope, exceptions, and competing rights can require legal analysis. Record facts that cut against your position as well as those that support it, including expired permissions, territorial limits, prior notices, or ongoing disputes.

## Check the required information and statements

Under [17 U.S.C. § 512(g)(3)](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512), an effective counter-notification includes substantially:

1. The subscriber's physical or electronic signature.
2. Identification of the removed or disabled material and the location where it appeared before removal.
3. A statement under penalty of perjury that the subscriber has a good-faith belief that the removal or disabling resulted from mistake or misidentification.
4. The subscriber's name, address, and telephone number.
5. The required statement consenting to federal district court jurisdiction and agreeing to accept service of process from the person who sent the original notice or that person's agent.

Read the provider's current instructions as well as the statute. The person named as the subscriber should personally review the contact details and required statements. Do not sign for another person unless you have confirmed the provider's requirements and your authority.

## Understand what happens after submission

The provider generally sends a compliant counter-notification to the original notice sender. Section 512 describes restoration of the material after no fewer than 10 and no more than 14 business days following receipt of the counter-notification, unless the provider first receives notice that the original claimant has filed an action seeking a court order concerning the material. Review the [U.S. Copyright Office's Section 512 overview](https://www.copyright.gov/512/) for the official explanation.

That statutory framework does not guarantee restoration in every platform process or resolve the underlying copyright dispute. Keep the submitted version, delivery confirmation, provider reference, and every later response. Distinguish an acknowledgment from a decision and avoid promising a fixed outcome or date.

## Consider disclosure and legal risk

A counter-notification contains contact and jurisdiction statements and may be shared with the original complainant. Section 512(f) also addresses liability for knowingly making a material misrepresentation that material was removed or disabled by mistake or misidentification. These are meaningful consequences, particularly when ownership, permission, or fair use is disputed.

Consult qualified legal counsel before proceeding if you are uncertain about the required statements, court jurisdiction, possible service of process, or the merits of the dispute. Administrative preparation cannot determine whether a use is lawful or replace legal representation.

## Prepare a reviewable file

Before opening the provider's form, assemble:

- The complete removal notice, case number, and provider correspondence
- A copy of the removed material and its former direct URL
- Account-holder and subscriber details that match the provider's records
- Licenses, permissions, source files, publication history, and relevant agreements
- A factual chronology of publication, notice, removal, and later communications
- Notes about known disputes, restrictions, or legal proceedings
