A DMCA notice is a formal copyright reporting mechanism, not a general complaint about unwanted content. Before preparing one, identify the work, your authority, and the exact online locations involved. This guide explains the main information categories; it is not a fill-in notice or individualized legal advice.

## The statutory information categories

For the notice-and-takedown process addressed by 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3)(A), the notice includes substantially these elements:

1. A physical or electronic signature from a person authorized to act for the owner of the exclusive right allegedly infringed.
2. Identification of the copyrighted work, or a representative list when multiple works at a single online site are covered.
3. Identification of the material to be removed or disabled and information sufficient for the provider to locate it.
4. Information sufficient to contact the complaining party, such as an address, telephone number, and available email address.
5. A statement of good-faith belief that the complained-of use is not authorized by the owner, its agent, or law.
6. A statement that the notice information is accurate and, under penalty of perjury, that the complaining party is authorized to act for the owner of the relevant exclusive right.

Read the requirements directly in [17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3)(A), published by the U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512). Do not sign statements you cannot truthfully confirm.

## Prepare records before opening the reporting channel

Create a working list of the original works and reported URLs. Give each reported location its own row, with a short explanation of the connection to the original. A general homepage or a screenshot without a location can leave a reviewer unable to find the relevant material.

Keep the claimant’s contact details and authorization records together. If an organization owns the rights, confirm who can approve the notice and respond to questions. Check that the name used in correspondence is consistent with the rights records, or explain the relationship clearly.

Preserve copies of your evidence and a version of the information approved for submission. Avoid adding unrelated allegations or personal details. The initial file should be understandable to someone who has not spoken with you.

## Verify the route and the statements

Use the provider’s official copyright reporting instructions. Confirm the destination and review the required fields at the time of submission. A reporting channel for copyright may differ from one for trademarks or impersonation. Do not assume that submitting one category also opens a report in another.

Review known permissions, licenses, and relevant context before confirming the statements. Ask qualified counsel about uncertain exceptions or competing rights. Administrative help with organizing a notice does not replace that assessment.

## What happens after submission

Keep the submitted version, submission date, and any acknowledgment or reference number. Record requests for clarification separately from enforcement decisions. Check which specific URLs a response covers, especially when several items were included.

The statute also provides a counter-notification process, and knowing material misrepresentations can carry liability. See [17 U.S.C. § 512(f) and (g)](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512). Obtain qualified legal advice promptly if a claim is disputed; this guide does not calculate deadlines or recommend litigation steps.

A notice does not guarantee that content will be removed or that an account will be suspended. The provider controls its decision.
